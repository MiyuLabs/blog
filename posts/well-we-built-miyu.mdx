## Wrecking The Brains

So, we had a plan. We had the components. We had a breadboard that was slowly becoming a crime scene. Now came the fun part. Actually making the damn thing work.

And this is where I started realizing that hardware has a very different definition of the word *"limited."*

Coming from software, I'm used to having resources that are... well, kinda stupidly abundant.
- Need more memory? Allocate some.
- Need more storage? Add a disk.
- Need more compute? Spin up a bigger machine.
- Want another service? Sure, here's another container.

The tiny microcontrollers does not give a shit about any of that. It has finite flash. Finite RAM. Finite CPU time. Finite GPIOs. Finite bandwidth. And then you look at everything we wanted Miyu to do and go:

>*"Yeah, this should be fine."*<br />— famous last words (not me), 2026

Trust me, it was not fine. We were building on an ESP-WROOM-32, and somehow our innocent little desk companion had already turned into a surprisingly complicated system. There was a display to drive, touch input to deal with, an SD card for assets, animations to render, audio to play, buttons to process, multiple apps to switch between, networking, Wi-Fi, background tasks... and eventually a whole backend sitting on the other side of the internet. And if it was less, everything still needed to be secure. TLS was the obvious choice we went for, that without saying, added extra memory footprint. All of that had to coexist on one tiny microcontroller. And that's where the actual brain damage began.

### When 240MHz Doesn't Feel Like Enough

One of the first things we had to understand was that *"it runs"* and *"it runs properly"* are two completely different things.

The display was probably the biggest offender. We were pushing a 480×320 display over SPI. On paper, that sounds pretty straightforward. In practice, every extra thing you wanted to do with the display had consequences somewhere else. And then there was touch. We initially had to figure out how the display and touch controller were going to coexist on the same hardware without stepping on each other's toes.

Eventually, we also made a conscious tradeoff around display read operations because we wanted the touch system to remain reliable. Even though we don't support touch, yet. But we've configured it in such a way that it's always possible to provide the update over OTA in future, if needed.

Basically, every time we thought, *"Oh, we'll just do this."* There was another tiny hardware limitation sitting in the corner whispering: *"Respectfully, fu\*k you! How about no?"*

Animations were another rabbit hole. We wanted Miyu to actually *feel* alive. Not just display a static image with a clock slapped beside it. Her little expressions, idle animations, reactions and all those tiny visual details were a huge part of what made her feel like a companion. But animations are basically a fancy way of saying, *"Please move a lot of pixels very quickly."* And the ESP32 had very different opinions about that.

We ended up working with dual frame buffering and optimizing how animation frames were read and displayed. But then memory usage and SD card access started becoming their own problems. At one point, the SD card mutex decided it wanted to participate in the group project titled *"how to annoy tf outta him?"* too.

Synchronization bugs are fun. Especially when everything *looks* correct.
You press something. It works.
You press it again. It works.
You let an animation run for a while. And suddenly... *"why the fuck is the cat looking like a frozen dog?"*

Then you spend hours staring at logs, changing timings, checking mutexes, moving things around, only to discover that two completely innocent-looking operations were the mastermind behind the war, fighting over the same resource. Classic.

### Audio Decided To Join The Fight

Then there was audio *(what the holy Music Player plays.)* We initially went with a PAM8403 amplifier because, well, it looked like a perfectly reasonable choice + it was super cheap *(yea honestly the primary reason was this xD)*.

It was not. Or rather, *it worked*. Which was almost worse. We could get audio out of it, but there was this lovely collection of analog noise and interference that made the whole thing sound like Miyu was trying to communicate with us from another dimension. Well, at one point I thought of marketing it as a feature: *static ambient noises that typical "slowed+reverb lofi songs" on youtube have*. But nah, it was against my pride.

So we debugged. And tested. And changed things. And tested again.

Eventually, instead of continuing to fight the analog setup, we decided to move to a MAX98357A digital I²S amplifier. Which worked much better. It also meant another component, another delivery, another bit of money disappearing from our pockets.

> *"It's only one more component." *<br />— famous last words, yk who.

And this was also where the capacitor joke from earlier came back around. We learned that capacitors aren't just mysterious cylindrical things you put on a board because the circuit diagram told you to. They actually become pretty damn useful when you're dealing with noisy power-hungry peripherals.

- Wi-Fi activity could introduce current spikes.
- Displays could behave weirdly.
- Audio could pick up jitter and noise.

Adding the right decoupling and smoothing capacitors in the right places helped absorb those little current fluctuations and stabilize things. So yes.

### The Optimization Hell

One thing I'm genuinely proud of from this whole process is how much we had to optimize. Our firmware ended up being around the ~1.2 MB range while running on hardware with only a few hundred kilobytes of usable memory and a 240 MHz dual-core processor. And yet it had to handle all of this simultaneously:

- Display rendering.
- Animation playback.
- Touch. Buttons. Audio.
- SD card operations.
- Wi-Fi. Networking.
- Multiple application states.
- Background synchronization.
- And eventually communication with our backend.

You can't add another CPU. You can't spin up another server and move the expensive part somewhere else. The hardware is sitting there, staring directly at you, saying: **"This is all you've got. Figure it out."** And honestly? I loved it. This is where the last +2 stages comes in, namely, *"UX & Design Aesthetics"* & *Optimizations & Bug Fixes*.

It forced me to actually think about what the code was doing.
- What was allocated.
- What could be reused.
- What needed to stay in memory.
- What could be streamed.
- What could be cached.
- How often something actually needed to run.
- Whether a particular operation was worth its cost.

*Oh fuck, the heap is fragmenting internally, panik. How do I fix this?* Every time a connection opens, encrypting it with mbedTLS adds ~40KB memory overhead. If you're already low on available heap, whooosh, *BROWNOUT*. All those things that are easy to ignore when you're writing software for a machine with absurd amounts of resources suddenly become very real when your entire application has to fit inside a tiny embedded system.

There were plenty of moments where we had to make compromises too. Sometimes the technically *best* solution wasn't practical given the memory, bandwidth, power, cost or development time available to us. So we picked the solution that actually worked. Because at the end of the day, we're building a product, not trying to win an optimization benchmark.

Though one caveat, for now we're letting MQTT communicate over plaintext. *Reason?* I already explained above, enabling TLS for MQTT on the current hardware introduces enough memory overhead to make the tradeoff difficult.. So for now, **Do NOT communicate any secret/confidential information via the partner messaging feature. It isn't even designed for that eitherways**. If you still do not get it, then you probably need to take the *Internet Etiquettes 101* Class again. But you get my point. *We ain't a banking organization that we're entitled to take care of it xD.* Though maybe, we'll address it in future.

### From One Little Device To A Whole System

While we had already built a functional proof-of-concept system to let Miyu communicate over the internet, scalable production infrastructure was an entirely different beast. Still, I'll narrate it like we were building it from scratch for the sake of my sanity. Now again, I definitely can't *(well, technically I indeed can, but I choose not to :p)* reveal all the internal infra-secrets :3, so don't get your hopes high. But if you're curious, here's something that'll definitely feel like a treat.

And before any itchy-fingered pentester try to find loopholes and exploit our network, trust me, there's not a single day that goes by where I don't think about you, ffs. So, take a chill-pill and relax &lt;3

Now coming back to the topic, the ESP32 obviously wasn't going to be directly connected to our database. That would be... let's just say, **a spectacularly bad idea.** We needed a proper device-facing backend. Something that could authenticate devices, manage their state, handle provisioning, synchronize data, deal with firmware updates and act as the controlled gateway between our little ESP32 and everything else.

For that, we went with **Go**. Mostly because I wanted something lightweight, predictable and fast enough that I wouldn't have to worry about the backend itself becoming the bottleneck. And also because I genuinely enjoy writing Go. There's something strangely satisfying about having a little Go binary sitting there quietly doing its job without demanding a whole universe of dependencies.

The architecture slowly started taking shape:
- The device communicates with the backend over HTTPS for normal API operations.
- The backend talks to PostgreSQL for persistent data.
- Firmware binaries and other larger assets live in Cloudflare R2 rather than being shoved into the database like some sort of psychopath.
- And for the partner system — where one Miyu needs to communicate with another Miyu in real time — we introduced MQTT.

That gave us a nice separation between *request/response* communication and *real-time events*.

And suddenly, our tiny desk companion had a scalable backend.

![MiyuLabs System Architecture](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MiyuLabs/blog/main/assets/well-we-built-miyu/device-network-architecture.png)

This architecture was probably one of the first moments where I looked at the project and went: **Oh. I'm actually building a system.** The device doesn't need to know where the database is. It doesn't need to know how messages are stored. It doesn't need to know where firmware binaries physically live. It doesn't need to know anything about authentication logic beyond what it needs to authenticate itself. It just needs to know:

- *"I have something to tell the backend."*
- *"I need something from the backend."*

Everything else happens behind the API. And that separation became extremely important.

### Making Two Miyus Talk

The partner system was probably the most interesting part of the backend. We wanted two Miyus to be able to communicate with each other in real time. So, imagine: Miyu A sends a little message. If Miyu B is online, it should receive that message almost immediately. If Miyu B isn't online?

Well... we can't exactly shout into the internet and hope she hears it later. So the message needs to be stored. This is where MQTT and the backend started working together. MQTT handles the real-time communication layer. Devices subscribe to their relevant topics and publish events through the broker. The backend handles the actual application logic and persistence. So a simplified flow looks something like:

**Miyu A → API → DB → MQTT → Miyu B**

If B is online, she gets the message in real time. If she's offline, the backend keeps the message around and she can synchronize it later over HTTPS.

This sounds pretty straightforward when written like that. It wasn't. There are a surprisingly large number of ways to screw up something as simple as *"send this message to another device."*

- What if the device disconnects halfway through?
- What if the message gets delivered but the acknowledgement never reaches the server?
- What if the same message gets processed twice?
- What if the device comes online after being offline for three days?
- What if both devices are online but one has a terrible Wi-Fi connection?
- What if the broker goes down?
- What if the API is available but MQTT isn't?

And suddenly we're discussing message persistence, acknowledgements, synchronization, QoS, presence, retries and offline delivery.

For a cat. **A fucking cat.** I love software engineering.

### Not Everything Needs To Be Microservices™

One thing I was particularly conscious about was not overengineering this. It would've been very easy to look at all these components and start drawing some ridiculous architecture with twelve microservices, three message queues, Kubernetes, service meshes, five observability stacks and a guy whose entire job is restarting pods.

No. We're a tiny team. We don't need to simulate Google. The architecture should be complicated **only where the problem itself is complicated**.

So the device-facing backend stayed as a focused Go service.
- PostgreSQL handles persistent application data.
- Mosquitto handles MQTT.
- Cloudflare R2 handles object storage.
- And the rest stays relatively boring.

And boring infrastructure is honestly underrated. If something doesn't need to be distributed, don't distribute it. If something doesn't need a queue, don't add a queue. If something doesn't need Kubernetes, please for the love of god don't add Kubernetes. We wanted something we could understand completely, deploy ourselves, debug ourselves and maintain without needing an entire infrastructure team. ~~*systemd ftw*~~. The goal wasn't to build the most impressive architecture diagram. The goal was to build something that actually works.

### And Then There Was The Website

Of course, Miyu needed a home on the internet too. And by this point, the website wasn't just going to be some static landing page. We had:

And honestly, this was one of those decisions that made the whole thing much easier to reason about. The public website and dashboard don't need to know anything about the internal device infrastructure beyond the interfaces they actually need. The browser talks to the web application. The web application handles the request. The appropriate backend logic does its thing. The database stores what needs storing. The device-facing infrastructure remains its own thing. Again, separation without unnecessary complexity.

> Of course, before anyone notices, the frontend fucking wasted a lot of my time. That's eventually what led to the delayed product launch. But welp, somehow it happened, heh.

### Cloudflare, My Beloved

Then we reached the internet-facing part. And this is where **Cloudflare, my beloved** entered the picture. Bro, it's so OG. It basically provides some pretty damn good infrastructure services for little to almost no money. And for our current use case, it's literally free. Because if you're putting a website on the public internet, you really don't want every request travelling directly to some little Linux server sitting somewhere and knocking on its door like: *"hello sir, another stranger from the internet has arrived."* So the architecture became:

**Browser → Cloudflare → Origin Server**

Cloudflare handles the edge. DNS. TLS. CDN. DDoS protection. WAF/security rules. Routing. And most importantly, **caching**.

But there was another thing I wanted to make explicit in the architecture: **caching doesn't only happen at one place.** There are effectively multiple layers involved:
- The browser can cache static resources.
- Cloudflare can cache content at the edge.
- And the origin itself can cache things through Nginx.

So we ended up thinking about it as a little **3-layer cache**: **Browser Cache → Edge Cache → Origin Cache**.

Obviously, not everything should be cached. Dynamic requests, authenticated requests and anything requiring fresh state need to reach the appropriate backend. But for things that *can* be cached? Why make the origin server do the same work again and again?

This was one of those things where the performance mindset from the ESP32 kind of carried over into the web architecture. On the ESP32, we were constantly asking: *"Do I really need to compute this again?"* On the web: *"Do I really need the origin to generate this again?"* Apparently my brain has one optimization strategy and it applies everywhere.

### The Origin Is Just A Little Linux Box

Behind Cloudflare sits our origin. A Linux server. Nginx. The Next.js application. PostgreSQL. Nothing particularly magical. And that's intentional.

Nginx acts as the reverse proxy and the first layer of traffic management on the server. It handles things like proxying requests to the Next.js application, security headers, rate limiting and origin-side caching. It can also act as a load balancer if we eventually run multiple application instances. So if we ever need to scale horizontally, we don't necessarily need to redesign the entire application architecture.

We can have something along the lines of:
**Cloudflare → Nginx → Next.js Instance(s)**

Instead of:
**Cloudflare → one poor Node process praying for its life.**

And PostgreSQL sits behind the application layer, accessible internally rather than being exposed directly to the public internet. That distinction matters.
- The browser doesn't get to talk to the database.
- The device doesn't get to talk to the database.
- Random internet people definitely don't get to talk to the database.

They talk to an application. The application decides what they're allowed to do. The database just does database things.

Simple.

### The Funny Thing About "Scalable"

There's something I learned while building all of this. People love using the word **scalable**.

*"Our architecture is scalable."*
*"We built a highly scalable microservice infrastructure."*
*"Our platform can scale to millions of users."*

Bro. We had barely launched. There weren't millions of users waiting outside the server room. What mattered to me wasn't designing something that could magically handle ten million requests per second. It was making sure that increasing usage wouldn't require throwing the whole system away. That's a much more useful definition of scalability for us.

Can I add another application instance? Can the cache absorb more traffic? Can static assets stay at the edge? Can the database remain private? Can the device backend scale independently from the website? Can MQTT handle the real-time device communication without forcing the web application to become a giant message broker? Can firmware files be served directly from object storage instead of our application server? Can we deploy a new version without manually SSH-ing into the machine at 3 AM?

If the answer is *yes*, we're probably doing something right. And if tomorrow we suddenly have way more traffic than expected, we'll deal with that problem **when it becomes a real problem**.

I don't want to spend six months preparing infrastructure for traffic that doesn't exist. And even if it did, the architecture is designed in such a way that we can easily scale up vertically and even horizontally.

### GitHub UwU

And then, of course, there was deployment. Because eventually you have to take the beautiful thing sitting on your laptop and somehow convince the production server that it should run it too. Initially, this was basically:

*"ssh into server → rsync source → install stuff → build → restart → pray."*

Which is fun exactly twice. After that, it's just annoying. So I built a clean CI/CD pipeline around GitHub Actions. The source code lives in GitHub. Push to `main`. GitHub Actions wakes up. Dependencies get installed. The application gets built. Tests/checks can run. The deployment happens over SSH. The new build reaches the origin server. And once deployment succeeds, the appropriate Cloudflare cache can be purged so the edge doesn't keep serving yesterday's version of the website.

Which is honestly one of those things that feels disproportionately satisfying.
You make a commit. Push. Watch the little workflow run. Green checkmark. Deployment complete. Cache purged. Website updated.

**uwu.**

*Okay, maybe I don't actually have this diagram in the article, but you get the idea. GitHub Actions is basically our little robot intern who never sleeps and doesn't ask for a salary. And before anyone pokes their nose around, yes, the CI/CD pipeline has proper separation between deploy and runtime permissions.*

### Designing For The Little Things Going Wrong

Another thing I started thinking about much more seriously was failure. Because when you're developing locally, everything works.
- Your Wi-Fi works.
- Your database is running.
- Your API is running.
- Your laptop is awake.
- Your device is sitting right next to the router.
- The sun is shining.
- Life is beautiful.

Production doesn't give a shit. Devices disconnect. Servers restart. Requests timeout. Networks become garbage. Caches become stale. Users close tabs halfway through requests. MQTT clients disappear. A deployment goes wrong. A firmware update gets interrupted. Someone enters something stupid into a form *(I call such fu*ked-up brainers, **"John"**. Sorry man, if your name is John. No hard feelings.)*. Someone inevitably tries to break something.

So instead of assuming everything is always available, the architecture had to account for things **not** working.
- That's why the device can synchronize over HTTPS.
- That's why partner messages can be persisted.
- That's why the MQTT layer isn't the only source of truth for message state.
- That's why firmware lives in object storage.
- That's why authentication exists at the application boundaries.
- That's why the database isn't publicly exposed.
- That's why caching is treated as a performance layer rather than the source of truth.

Basically: **Things that are destined not to break, will eventually break.** Design accordingly.

## Some Rantings?

Okay, now that we've reached the end... a few rants.

Building Miyu was probably one of the most exhausting things I've worked on. And also one of the most fun. Those two things somehow managed to coexist.

When you see the final product, it's really easy to look at it and think *"oh, that's pretty cool."* You don't see the countless tiny things that had to happen before that:
- You don't see the nights spent debugging something that should've worked.
- You don't see the hours spent reading the outdated garbage documentation for some obscure component.
- You don't see the prototype that didn't work.
- The PCB revision that needed changing.
- The design that didn't fit.
- The vendor that was rude.
- The budget that definitely didn't cooperate.
- The feature that sounded really cool but couldn't make the cut.
- And you definitely don't see the amount of time spent asking: *"Why the fuck isn't this working?"*.

Working on hardware also made me realize just how much I had taken software for granted. In software, you can usually just add another file. Another dependency. Another abstraction. Another server. Hardware doesn't care. You have a finite board. Finite pins. Finite power. And if two things don't physically fit together... well, good luck convincing them otherwise.

The crazy amount of optimizations I had to do at such a constrained compute & memory footprint was something I'm really proud of. And honestly, I love how much hardware forces you to respect resources. We routinely waste absurd amounts of memory and compute power writing software, then complain when systems with gigabytes of RAM feel slow. Meanwhile, a roughly **~1.2 MB firmware image**, running on a minimal microcontroller with just **~320 KB of DRAM** and a **240 MHz dual-core CPU**, can handle an absurd amount of multitasking. It's just mind blowing. And the amount of respect I have for electronics engineers & low-level developers have increased dramatically in my eyes :)

And then there's time. Studies don't stop. Exams don't care that your PCB just arrived. Deadlines don't care that you spent the entire night fixing a firmware bug. Life doesn't exactly pause because you have a cool project going on. There were times when the project felt like it was moving incredibly slowly. Times when we were frustrated. Times when we'd question whether we were even going to finish it. But somehow, we kept coming back to it. Maybe because we'd already invested too much. Maybe because we genuinely wanted to see it exist. Maybe because giving up would've been more annoying than continuing. Probably a combination of all three.

Doing this with a small team makes everything even more interesting. There's no dedicated hardware team. No firmware team. No backend team. No frontend team. No manufacturing department. It's just you. Which sounds cool until you realize that *"just you"* also means that when something breaks, **you** are the hardware team, firmware team, backend team, frontend team, deployment team, design team & support team simultaneously. But despite all that, I'm really glad that I had such cool teammates with me, who took care of all the non-tech-but-super-important stuff. So that *no black shadow of management-responsibility can fall upon thyself (me) xD*

And honestly, I think that's the biggest thing Miyu gave us. Not just the technical experience. Not just the hardware knowledge. Not just learning how to architect a production system or ship an actual product. Trust me I ain't gonna end with ~~"maybe the real Miyu is the friends we made along the way. if it was my personal blog, i totally would 😹"~~. It taught us what happens when you take an idea seriously enough to keep working on it even after the initial excitement wears off. The idea was fun, building it was hard. Finishing it was even harder. But seeing it actually exist? **WORTH ALL THAT EFFORT.**
